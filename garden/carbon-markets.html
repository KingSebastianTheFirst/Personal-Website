Carbon Markets & Asset Pricing

🌿 Growing

Cap-and-trade systems, carbon prices, and what high emissions actually cost in equity markets.

📌 What are carbon markets?

Carbon markets — formally known as emissions trading systems (ETS) or cap-and-trade schemes — are market mechanisms for pricing greenhouse gas emissions. A regulator sets a cap on total allowable emissions and issues permits accordingly. Firms that need to emit must hold permits; those that can reduce emissions cheaply can sell excess permits to higher-cost emitters. The price of permits is determined by supply and demand.

The EU ETS is the world's largest carbon market, covering around 40% of EU greenhouse gas emissions across energy, heavy industry, and aviation. The California Cap-and-Trade program and China's national ETS are other major examples. As of 2024, carbon pricing instruments cover roughly 24% of global GHG emissions.

💡 The carbon premium debate

If carbon markets work as intended, high-emission firms face higher costs and should earn lower equity returns (a "carbon discount") as investors price in their exposure. Alternatively, investors may demand a higher return from high-emission firms as compensation for bearing carbon transition risk (a "carbon premium"). The empirical literature has produced conflicting evidence on which effect dominates.

My research with Aryan Goswami provides a resolution: the relationship is state-dependent. The regime of carbon price uncertainty — not just the level — is the key variable. See below.

🔬 In my research

In "Carbon Price Volatility and Stock Returns" (with Aryan Goswami, 2026), we study whether uncertainty in carbon markets changes how equity markets price firm-level carbon exposure. We use a monthly panel of STOXX Europe 600 firms over 2013–2022, matched with firm-level GHG data, EU allowance prices, and the option-implied Carbon VIX (CVIX) — a measure of market-implied carbon price volatility.

Key findings:

  • In low-CVIX periods, high-emission firms trade at a modest discount (consistent with carbon discounting by investors).
  • In high-CVIX periods, the relationship reverses: a 10-point CVIX increase raises monthly excess returns for top emitters by ~0.30 percentage points.
  • The effect is present for Scope 1 (direct) and Scope 2 (energy-related) emissions and partially for Scope 3 (value chain) emissions.
  • The finding reconciles the conflicting evidence in the literature: both a positive and negative carbon premium can exist, depending on the volatility regime.

Practically, this means that static carbon screens — which treat carbon exposure as a constant risk factor — can systematically misstate expected returns, particularly during periods of climate policy uncertainty.
