Carbon Price Uncertainty

🌿 Growing

Why volatility in carbon markets matters as much as price levels — for investment, risk, and policy design.

📌 What is it?

Carbon price uncertainty refers to the volatility and unpredictability of prices in emissions trading markets. Even if we agreed on a long-run carbon price trajectory, the path to get there would be uncertain: policy changes, geopolitical shocks, technological surprises, and shifts in permit supply all create short-run volatility in carbon prices.

This uncertainty is distinct from the level of carbon prices. A carbon price of €60/tonne with low volatility and a price of €60/tonne with high volatility have very different implications for investment decisions, risk management, and the cost of holding carbon-intensive assets.

💡 Why does uncertainty matter separately from price level?

Standard asset pricing theory tells us that investors demand higher returns for risks that are more uncertain. If carbon transition risk becomes more uncertain — not necessarily more severe in expectation, just harder to predict — rational investors should require a higher return premium to hold carbon-exposed assets.

This has a counterintuitive implication: during periods of elevated carbon market volatility, high-emission firms may actually become more attractive to hold (higher expected returns) rather than less, as investors demand compensation for bearing the uncertainty. This is the "risk compensation" mechanism, and it can dominate the "carbon penalty" effect during volatile periods.

🔬 In my research

The paper "Carbon Price Volatility and Stock Returns" (with Aryan Goswami, 2026) tests this mechanism empirically using the option-implied Carbon VIX (CVIX) — an index measuring market participants' expectations of future carbon price volatility in the EU ETS. Key findings:

  • High-emission firms earn a modest discount during low-CVIX regimes.
  • During high-CVIX periods, the relationship reverses and top emitters earn significantly higher excess returns.
  • The CVIX therefore acts as a regime-switching mechanism, determining the sign and magnitude of the carbon premium.
  • The results are robust to alternative emission measures (Scope 1, 2, 3) and multiple robustness checks.

This finding has direct implications for portfolio management: carbon screens that treat emission exposure as a constant factor — always a penalty or always neutral — will systematically misprice assets during high-uncertainty episodes.

💭 Policy implications

For policymakers, the result underscores the value of predictable, stable carbon pricing over volatile or unpredictable pricing. If carbon price uncertainty itself creates financial distortions — inflating returns to carbon-intensive firms during volatile periods — then policy design that reduces uncertainty (long-term price floors, stable permit supply rules) may be valuable not just for investment certainty but for market functioning.
