EU Taxonomy

🌳 Evergreen

Europe's classification system for environmentally sustainable economic activities.

📌 What is it?

The EU Taxonomy is a classification system developed by the European Union that defines which economic activities can be considered environmentally sustainable. Rather than leaving "green" as a vague label, the Taxonomy sets out specific, science-based technical screening criteria for activities that make a substantial contribution to one of six environmental objectives — primarily climate change mitigation and adaptation.

For an activity to be Taxonomy-aligned, it must: (1) make a substantial contribution to at least one of the six environmental objectives; (2) do no significant harm to the other five; and (3) meet minimum social safeguards. Companies subject to EU disclosure requirements must report the share of their revenues, capital expenditure, and operating expenditure that is Taxonomy-aligned.

💡 Why it matters

The Taxonomy matters because sustainable finance depends on a shared definition of "sustainable." Without one, ESG ratings diverge — different providers assess the same company as green or brown based on entirely different methodologies. This creates uncertainty for investors, makes capital allocation inefficient, and allows greenwashing to persist.

The Taxonomy also matters politically: it is the European Commission's attempt to redirect significant capital flows toward climate-compatible activities. If firms know that Taxonomy-aligned activities attract cheaper capital, they have a real financial incentive to transition. That transmission channel only works if the Taxonomy is credible, consistent, and actually used.

🔬 In my research

In a paper with Maurice Dumrose and Julia Eckert, published in Finance Research Letters (2022), we were among the first to use actual Taxonomy alignment data — from ISS ESG — to study whether the Taxonomy relates to how firms are rated by major ESG providers. Using tobit regressions on a sample of 1,813 firms and ratings from MSCI, S&P, Refinitiv, and V.E (Moody's), we find:

  • Taxonomy alignment is significantly positively correlated with environmental (E) ratings from three of four providers.
  • The relationship is stronger for firms with full Taxonomy exposure (revenues entirely within defined Taxonomy activities).
  • S&P's E ratings are not significantly related to Taxonomy alignment in our main specification — potentially reflecting differences in rating methodology.
  • The potential for reducing measurement divergence has not fully materialized yet.

This suggests that the Taxonomy is already beginning to anchor ESG measurement — but its influence remains partial and uneven across providers.
