ESG Ratings

🌳 Evergreen

What they measure, why they disagree, and what that means for capital markets.

📌 What are they?

ESG ratings are assessments of a company's performance across Environmental (E), Social (S), and Governance (G) dimensions. Provided by specialist agencies — MSCI, S&P Global (formerly RobecoSAM), Sustainalytics, Refinitiv, Moody's V.E, ISS ESG, and others — they are used by investors to construct sustainable portfolios, by banks to assess counterparty risk, and increasingly by regulators to monitor market practices.

In principle, ESG ratings should help capital flow toward more responsible, resilient companies. In practice, the ratings from different providers for the same company often look startlingly different.

💡 The divergence problem

Unlike credit ratings, which correlate highly across providers (Moody's and S&P agree on investment grade versus junk most of the time), ESG ratings can diverge dramatically. The academic literature refers to this as "aggregate confusion." The divergence stems from two main sources:

  • Scope divergence: Providers disagree on which attributes define sustainability — some focus on climate, others on labour rights, governance structures, or supply chain practices.
  • Measurement divergence: Even when providers agree on what to measure, they use different data sources and scoring methods, yielding different numbers.

This divergence has real consequences. When investors face contradictory ratings, they cannot efficiently allocate capital based on sustainability. It also creates space for firms to manage perceptions — improving ratings without necessarily improving underlying performance.

🔬 In my research

Two strands of my research touch directly on ESG ratings and their limitations.

First, in the paper with Dumrose and Eckert on the EU Taxonomy (Finance Research Letters, 2022), we examine whether the Taxonomy's science-based definition of environmental sustainability can reduce divergence across E ratings. We find positive evidence — Taxonomy alignment correlates with higher E ratings from three of four major providers — but the harmonizing effect remains partial.

Second, in the paper with Fabisik on perceived versus objective corporate greenness (2026), we look at the user side of sustainability data: even if ESG data is available, do people actually interpret it correctly? The answer is no — people systematically misread emissions data in ways that attenuate the market signal. This is a form of ESG data failure that operates downstream of the rating agencies entirely.
